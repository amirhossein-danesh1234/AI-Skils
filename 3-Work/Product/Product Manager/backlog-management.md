# Backlog Management

## Purpose

Keep product work discoverable, coherent, and ready for the next decision.

## When to Use

A backlog contains stale, duplicate, ambiguous, or unowned items.

## When Not to Use

Do not equate a large backlog with a roadmap or delete user work without authority.

## Required Inputs

### Required

Existing backlog, item history, product goals, owners, and team workflow.

### Helpful

User segment and scenario, evidence, business objective, constraints, current behavior, relevant policies, and decision owner.

### Optional

Previous decisions, comparable cases, or preferred output format. Their absence must not block a bounded first pass.

If a required fact is missing, identify which decision it affects. Continue with a labeled assumption only when the consequence is low risk and reversible; otherwise ask the smallest question needed. Do not invent project facts, approvals, measurements, or test results.

## Output

Maintained backlog with clear status, links, ownership, readiness gaps, and explicit archive proposals.

## Operating Principles

Maintain traceability from problem to scope to acceptance. Reject weak requests with reasons and a better alternative; distinguish useful outcomes from shipped output.

Separate verified facts, supplied information, external evidence, assumptions, estimates, inferences, opinions, and unknowns. Show the basis of consequential claims. Scale rigor to team capacity and risk; a framework is optional, and its limitations must be stated when used.

## Workflow

1. Inspect item sources and distinguish ideas, evidence tasks, committed work, and defects.
2. Link duplicates and dependencies while preserving useful history and identifiers.
3. Define lightweight readiness and aging rules appropriate to the team.
4. Refresh top candidates, record dispositions for stale items, and route unresolved decisions.

## Decision Rules

- If an item has no current value or owner, propose archive or revalidation rather than silent deletion.
- If refinement cost exceeds likely value, retain a concise idea until priority rises.

## Validation

- Can the team identify the next ready work and why it matters?
- Are status meanings consistent and commitments separated from possibilities?

## Common Failure Modes

- Refining every item deeply: focus on near-term decisions.
- Duplicate tickets create double commitments: link and resolve ownership.

## Escalation and Collaboration

Scrum Master facilitates refinement; Project Manager tracks commitments; Product Strategist checks relevance.

Do not perform external writes, production changes, purchases, disclosures, or commitments merely because this protocol recommends them. Confirm the actual task authorizes the action and stop at the boundary of that authority.

## Completion Criteria

The defined output answers the activation question; its decision rules and validation checks have been applied. Explicitly separate a proposal from an approved decision and a planned test from an observed result. Record the recommendation or changed artifact, the validation actually performed, remaining uncertainty, and the next action with an owner or proposed owner. Include priority, dependency, and definition of done when work remains. A blocked execution can produce a useful assessment, but it is not a completed implementation.
